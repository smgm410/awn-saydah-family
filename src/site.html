This is the story of how the Aouns of Damour and the Saydahs of Forzul crossed an ocean, settled in Brooklyn, and became the family we are today. It is built from ship lists, census pages, naturalization papers, death certificates and newspaper clippings, and from the stories our family has told. Every record sits right beside the part of the story it belongs to. This is a first edition, not the last word, and it will keep growing as new records turn up.

A note for Mom, Aunt Jackie, Aunt Stephanie and Aunt Kathy

This is your parents' story, Mama Helen and Tony's, and you are the ones who carry it. Much of what is in here began with something one of you remembered or kept, such as the memorial cards Aunt Jackie shared. If something looks wrong, or you remember something a record could never tell us, please tell me. I would love to add it.

How to read it

Every fact carries one of three labels:

  • Confirmed: a record shows it.

  • Family: told to us, not yet found in a record.

  • Probable: it fits the records but is not proven.

Names changed shape on the way. The family name Aoun became Awn (Family), and records also write it Owen and Aun. Saydah appears as Saidah and Sayda. These are the same families, and each time we say which spelling a record used.

What I would love from you: corrections, names for the faces in old photographs, and the stories no record keeps. Send Sarah a correction, a name or a memory and it will be added here, so this story keeps growing for the grandchildren and great-grandchildren.

Part OneThe Aoun (Awn) Family, from Damour to Brooklyn

What we foundThe family was Aoun in Damour. The family says the name became Awn in America (Family), and records also wrote it Owen and Aun; Joseph’s death certificate says Owen (Confirmed).

The Awn story begins in Damour, a coastal village south of Beirut.

Damour sits on the Mediterranean coast about eleven miles south of Beirut. When the Aouns left in the summer of 1920, the First World War had only just ended, and Mount Lebanon was recovering from a wartime famine that, by most historians' estimates, killed a large share of its people. In September 1920, as the family crossed into New York State, France was proclaiming the new state of Greater Lebanon under its mandate. This is background, not a record of this family, and the papers do not tell us why Joseph and Assin decided to go. What they do tell us is that they took all eight children with them, a party of ten on third-class tickets by way of Southampton and Quebec, with Joseph about 50 years old and an uncle waiting in Brooklyn. That is a lot to stake on a new country.

Joseph and Assin Aoun

What we foundJoseph’s death certificate says “Joseph K. Owen,” but the Arabic papers call him Joseph Khoury, son of Boutros, so the K. stands for Khoury (Confirmed).

Leaving Damour with eight children

In the summer of 1920, Joseph Aoun was about fifty. He took his wife Assin and eight children aboard on third-class tickets, by way of Southampton and Quebec, bound for an uncle, Michal Malleut, who was waiting in Brooklyn. The youngest, Anthony, was six. He would become our grandfather (Confirmed: the 1920 ship manifest). Family memory: why they left, or what the trip was like. If you know, please tell me.

They settled in Brooklyn. By 1932 Joseph was retired. Between them Joseph and Assin buried a son, Nassib, in 1924, and another, George, in 1930. They had also left a daughter, Esther, behind in Lebanon (Confirmed: death certificates and the Arabic death notices of 1930 and 1935).

Joseph died on 14 September 1932. Assin died on 16 October 1935 at Bay Ridge Hospital, and her son John signed for the burial permit (Confirmed).

The details: spellings, certificates and what the Arabic papers add

Anthony's father, Joseph Aoun, was born 21 November 1869 and died in Brooklyn at about 1 a.m. on 14 September 1932, aged 62. His death certificate spells the family name Owen, lists him as retired, and gives his parents as Peter and Teresa. The cause was cardiac failure from a coronary thrombosis, and he was buried at St. John's Cemetery on 16 September (Confirmed, NYC death certificate no. 18405). On the 1920 ship manifest he is Youseuf Aoun, a farmer or traveler of 53. The certificate says he had lived in the U.S. for 12 years, which fits the 1920 arrival.

His wife Assin Aoun (written Essine on the manifest and Aseen in the 1925 census) was the daughter of Joseph Segan (Segen; Sajaan in Arabic) and Susanne, whose family name reads Nahmir on the certificate (Probable). She died on Wednesday 16 October 1935 at Bay Ridge Hospital, a widow living at 1047 75th Street, of a ruptured appendix after an emergency appendectomy (Al-Hoda also says appendicitis). The Arabic notices call her a woman from Damour. A Mass was said for her at Our Lady of Lebanon on Friday 18 October, the day she was buried at St. John’s, and her son John signed for the burial permit (Confirmed: certificate; Mira’at al-Gharb, 18 October, and Al-Hoda, 16 to 19 October 1935; one line in Al-Hoda names St. Joseph’s church instead). Her age is written four different ways across the records (45, 40, 50 and 52), so her birth year is unknown. Her death record also spells the name Owen (Confirmed). The same undertaker, Jere J. Cronin, Inc., on Atlantic Avenue, handled the funerals of Joseph, Assin and their son Nassib.

Assin’s notice also names a circle of close relatives: the sons of Habib Aoun (Sajaan, Khaleel, Farid, Sa’id and Shaker) and the Aql family. Exactly how they were related to Joseph or Assin is not yet known (Probable as kin only). Joseph lies in St. John’s Cemetery, Section 3, Row C, Grave 4A, while Assin and her son George share Section 20, Row S, Grave 207; why they are apart is not known (Find a Grave).

The Arabic-language newspapers of the day add what the certificates leave out. (Our English readings of these notices are our own translations and still need a native Arabic reader to confirm.) They write Joseph’s name as Joseph Khoury, son of Boutros (Peter), so the K. on his death certificate stands for Khoury, and they say he was from Damour (Confirmed). They report that he died on the morning of Wednesday 14 September 1932 of a heart attack, with the Mass on Friday 16 September (Confirmed: Al-Hoda, 14 to 17 September 1932). Where the funeral was held is not settled: the Brooklyn Times Union (item 133) says St. Mary’s Syrian Roman Catholic Church, while the Arabic papers say Our Lady of Lebanon. Joseph had two brothers: Michel, who stayed in Damour, and Gabriel, who died in Brazil in about early 1926 (Confirmed: Ash-Shams, 1 March 1926).

The crossing, 1920

Ten people, one ship

On 25 August 1920 the Aoun family left Southampton on the S.S. Grampian, bound for Quebec. They went on by train and crossed into New York State at Rouses Point on 5 September 1920 (Confirmed: the ship’s manifest, Anthony’s immigration card and his own petition).

The manifest lists the household together, heading for the uncle, Michal Malleut, in Brooklyn, with Damour as their last home. Joseph is written Youseuf, 53, and Assin Essine, 45. Ship ages are approximate: Anthony is 4 on the manifest, but 6 by his birth date (Confirmed: names; Family: that all eight are brothers and sisters).

Home on Tompkins Place

A house full of working children

By 1925 the family lived at 37 Tompkins Place in Brooklyn. The state census that year lists nine people at home, all born in Syria: Joseph, Assin and seven of their children. Nassib was missing, because he had died there the October before (Confirmed: 1925 state census).

By April 1930 Mathilde had married and moved out. John was a manager in a dry goods business, Theresa a clerk in dry goods, George a manager in ladies’ apparel and Mitchell a clerk (Confirmed: 1930 census). The family says only Anthony and Mitchell were allowed to go to school while the others worked (Family). A graduate list of 1932 names an Anthony Aoun, who may be ours (Probable).

Becoming American

The brothers each took the same path to citizenship, and the papers survive:

  • Elias: declaration of intention 1937; petition and oath with witnesses 1941.

  • Mitchell: declaration 1939; WWII draft card 1940; petition; and a name-change order in 1943.

  • John: declaration 1940; petition and oath with witnesses 1942.

  • Anthony: declaration 1939; draft card 1940; petition and oath on 10 November 1942. See Part Three.

The brothers' and sisters' later lives

John J. Awn (Jean on the manifest, and John Joseph Owen when he signed his name) was the oldest son in the household after Nassib’s death. He first filed to become a citizen in 1926, filed again in 1940 as a merchant living at 1047 75th Street, and took the oath on 5 November 1942, with Said Owen, a shoemaker, and his brother-in-law Fred Gorayeb as witnesses (Confirmed, naturalization papers).

His birth date puzzled even the courts. In December 1940 the Brooklyn Eagle reported that he asked a federal court to change it from 15 August 1905 to 15 August 1903, and that the Assistant U.S. Attorney opposed the change as an attempt to avoid the draft. Later papers use 4 August 1903, which also fits his ages in the 1925 and 1930 censuses.

Mitchell Awn was born 4 July 1909 in Damour. He married Barbara Machanskis on 14 October 1940 in Yonkers (Confirmed, his draft card). On 9 June 1943 a federal court changed his name from Mitchell Joseph Khoury Aoun to Mitchell Awn, the order that made Awn his legal name (Confirmed). Part TwoThe Saydah Family, from Forzul to Brooklyn

Helen's father, Antoon (W.) Saydah, came from Forzul, Lebanon. We call him Antoon, which is how the Arabic papers and his 1942 draft card write his first name; many records write it Anton, and some Antoun. His family name appears as Saydah, Saidah, Sayda, Saida and even Smyda. Helen's mother was born a Hadad (Wassela Hadad), a name that records also spell Hodod, Haddad and Hadel.

Antoon arrives, 1898

A young man from Forzul

Antoon Saydah was born in Forzul on 15 August, in 1880 or 1881. By his own sworn word, he left Le Havre on the liner La Touraine in October 1898 and reached New York on 25 October, still in his teens (Confirmed: his 1909 declaration and 1913 petition, as his own statement). Family memory: stories of the voyage or why he left. If you know, please tell me.

Dead Ends and Ruled-Out Records

Part of good research is knowing which records are not ours. These are written down so nobody has to search for them twice.

  • Joseph Owen, who died 27 April 1932 at 385 Clinton Street, is not Anthony's father. He was a grocer of 55 whose wife was Sadie and whose parents were Michael and Mary, and he had been in the U.S. about 18 years. Our Joseph died in September 1932, aged 62, married to Assin, with parents Peter and Teresa, and had arrived in 1920. The same cemetery (St. John's) and the same undertaker (Jere J. Cronin, Inc.) buried both, but Owen was a common spelling among Brooklyn Syrian families and nothing ties the two men together (Certain: a different man).

  • A "John Khouri" fruit-store lead is not John Awn (Family, from Sarah).

  • A 1906 petition by a Salim Haddad (born 1883, in the U.S. since 1900, living on Washington Street in Manhattan) and a 1897 Kansas City card for a Salim Hadad are Probably different men from Wassela's brother Salim, who was in Brooklyn by 1892 and in the June 1900 census.

  • The January 1898 ship card for ten-year-old "Wassilah Hadad" Probably describes a different girl, because the Hadads were already living in Brooklyn.

  • The 1920 Ishpeming census page for an Assad Kirkish household (pictured in item 106) is Possible at best. Probably a different Kirkish family, but Assad's wife Sadie could be the "Sada Kirkish" named in the 1953 Eagle notice, so it stays open.

  • George Abraham Saydah of Bay Ridge (1880 to 1963) is not shown to be related. His 1923 declaration and 1926 petition (no. 68097) say he came from Cairo on the Prinzess Irene in August 1906 and lived on Ovington Avenue, with wife Melia and three children born in Hoboken. Nothing in the papers names Antoon, Khaleel or Michael, so he stays here until a record links him (Possible at best; FamilySearch, Brooklyn naturalizations, NARA image group 007784999, images 703, 705 and 706 of 1018, arks 3:1:3Q9M-CSMR-HQP4-4, -HQ2J-P and -HQP4-8).

Still Searching

A family history is never finished. These are the open questions. If you know anything about any of them, please use the sharing form below.

  • Anthony and Helen’s wedding. The date, Sunday 29 December 1940, is settled by two Arabic papers and Anthony’s petition. We would still like the marriage certificate, and the name of the church, which is hard to read in the newspaper scan.

  • George Awn. He drowned on 27 July 1930. We do not know where (Long Island or a lake in Rhode Island), why his age is given as 24, 22 and 21, or who the friend with him was. A death record or the New York papers of 28 and 29 July 1930 would settle it.

  • Anthony’s death certificate and Wassela’s 1969 death record. We have Anthony’s death notice (Home Reporter, 15 July 1966) but not his certificate. For Wassela there is a newspaper index entry (The Record of Hackensack, 8 January 1969) and a sympathy notice, but not the certificate.

  • Birth years. Was Antoon born in 1880 or 1881? And when was Wassela born? The 1900 census says 1886, while every later record says about 1889 to 1893. Is Zahleh really her birthplace? A second record for each would settle it.

  • The church. Anthony’s side was Maronite, at Our Lady of Lebanon. The notices say the bride was not Maronite, and the Saydahs used Presbyterian and Episcopal churches (Khaleel studied at a Presbyterian seminary, and Antoon’s funeral was at Christ Episcopal), so they look Protestant (Probable). Our Lady of Lebanon’s registers may say more.

  • Lebanon. We have not yet found records from Damour or Forzul. Parish and civil registers there could take us back past Joseph and Antoon.

  • Koury-Awn, Inc., at 38 East 32nd Street, was Anthony's employer in 1940. We would like to know who ran it, and whether Michael Koury (Theresa's husband) was in the clothing business.

  • Esther Ayoub's family in Beirut. The 1961 Caravan notice names her son Antoine Ayoub (with his wife and children) and her daughters Mrs. Sira San Remi and Mrs. Mary Awn of Beirut. It does not name a husband. Does anyone remember them?

  • The Tuxedo Park accident, 1916. Who was driving, Antoon or Khaleel? And how did the new trial of 1919 end? We would like Najib Maloof’s death record and the court file.

  • 73 Washington Street, 1898. The La Touraine entry for "Antoun Smyda" (if it is Antoon) says he was joining a brother at 73 Washington Street. That brother is Probably Khaleel, but we have not shown where Khaleel lived in 1898. A grocer named John Haddad is listed at 73 Washington Street in an 1898 directory, which is Possibly Wassela's father. Did the two families share the address?

  • Antoon's ship and arrival date. He swore he arrived on La Touraine on 25 October 1898. The only matching entry we found, "Antoun Smyda," 18, is on a La Touraine list dated 28 November 1898, and it may not be him. The year 1898 is supported by the 1900 and 1910 censuses, but his own name has not turned up on the 25 October sailing. We need that page, or a record that confirms or rules out the Smyda entry.

  • More open conflicts. Where Hanna Hadad died (Clifton Springs or “Willard”), where Antoon died (Allendale or Columbia, South Carolina), which church held Joseph’s funeral (St. Mary’s or Our Lady of Lebanon), where Sa’da Kirkish lived and who her husband was, and who the Najeeb Hadad of 4721 Sixth Avenue (died 13 January 1914) was.

  • Fred Gorayeb's death, and where he and Mathilde are buried. Mathilde Probably died in December 1984, from an index entry. We have found nothing on Fred's death.

  • Sada and Nustas Kirkish. The 1948, 1949 and 1953 notices for Khaleel, Antoon and Michael all name them as sisters. Sada is Probably "Sady," wife of Abdalla Kirkish, in whose Michigan household Antoon boarded in 1900. We have not yet found a record naming their parents. Michigan death certificates and the Kirkish censuses might.

  • Evelyn and Virginia in 1940. The 1940 census page we found lists only Antoon, Wassela and Helen, and the next sheet has not been checked. The sisters' marriages are known from newspapers and indexes (Virginia in 1945, Evelyn in 1954 and 1971), but we have not seen the marriage certificates.

  • Michael Saydah. Antoon's brother Michael lived in Chicago from about 1891 and moved to Brooklyn around 1916 to 1918 (Confirmed, from his passport applications and 1918 draft card). He Probably died on 1 March 1953 and was buried in Green-Wood Cemetery on 4 March. We do not know what became of his first wife, Marie Luckey (married in Grundy County, Illinois, in 1901), or whether he was born in 1877, as his own records say, or about 1870, as his sons' certificates imply. His 1903 naturalization papers (Cook County) may name his parents.

  • Mitchell Awn's two wives, Barbara Machanskis and Laurdes Nassr, and what became of Barbara.

  • Living memories. A conversation with family would fill in what no record keeps, such as Esther Ayoub's family and which brother had the arm surgery.
