This is the story of how the Aouns of Damour and the Saydahs of Forzul crossed an ocean, settled in Brooklyn, and became the family we are today. It is built from ship lists, census pages, naturalization papers, death certificates and newspaper clippings, and from the stories our family has told. Every record sits right beside the part of the story it belongs to. This is a first edition, not the last word, and it will keep growing as new records turn up.

A note for Anthony and Helen’s children

This is Anthony and Helen’s story (Tony and “Mama Helen” to their family), and their children are the ones who carry it. Much of what is in here began with something one of them remembered or kept, such as the memorial cards Jackie shared. If something looks wrong, or you remember something a record could never tell us, please tell Sarah, who would love to add it.

How to read it

Every fact on this site is one of three kinds:

  • A plain statement: a record shows it.

  • “The family says”: told to us, not yet found in a record.

  • “Probably”: it fits the records but is not proven.

A few words come up often. A petition for naturalization is the form a person filed to become a United States citizen. A passenger list is the ship’s list of the people who crossed. An index line is a typed listing that points to a record but is not the record itself.

Names changed shape on the way. The family name was Aoun; the family says it became Awn, and records also write it Owen and Aun. Saydah appears as Saidah and Sayda. These are the same families, and each time we say which spelling a record used.

What I would love from you: corrections, names for the faces in old photographs, and the stories no record keeps. Send Sarah a correction, a name or a memory and it will be added here, so this story keeps growing for the grandchildren and great-grandchildren.

Part OneThe Aoun (Awn) Family, from Damour to Brooklyn

What we foundThe family was Aoun in Damour. The family says the name became Awn in America, and records also wrote it Owen and Aun; Joseph’s death certificate says Owen.

The Awn story begins in Damour, a coastal village south of Beirut.

Damour sits on the Mediterranean coast about eleven miles south of Beirut. When the Aouns left in the summer of 1920, the First World War had only just ended, and Mount Lebanon was recovering from a wartime famine that, by most historians' estimates, killed a large share of its people. In September 1920, as the family crossed into New York State, France was proclaiming the new state of Greater Lebanon under its mandate. This is background, not a record of this family, and the papers do not tell us why Joseph and Assin decided to go. What they do tell us is that they took all eight children with them, a party of ten on third-class tickets by way of Southampton and Quebec, with Joseph about 50 years old and Brooklyn as their destination. That is a lot to stake on a new country.

What we foundThe man the city wrote down as “Joseph K. Owen” was Yusuf al-Khoury Butrus Awn to his own people. Yusuf is the Arabic form of Joseph, Butrus is Peter, and the K. most likely stands for Khoury (our translation, to be confirmed by a native reader).

“Death of a righteous man”Al-Hoda, 17 September 1932, p.3, the local-news headline over Joseph’s death (our translation, to be confirmed by a native reader)

When Joseph died in Brooklyn on 14 September 1932, the city recorded him as “Joseph K. Owen,” retired, aged 62, the son of Peter and Teresa Owen. The Arabic paper Al-Hoda printed what looks like another man: “Yusuf al-Khoury Butrus Awn,” of Damour (our translation, to be confirmed by a native reader). He is one person, and the two names fit together. The ship list of 1920 had already written him a third way, Youseuf Aoun, a farmer of 53 (a second word is crossed out).

His death certificate says he was born on 21 November 1869, and that he had lived in the United States for 12 years, which fits the family’s arrival in 1920. He died of cardiac failure with a coronary thrombosis and was buried at St. John’s Cemetery on 16 September. Al-Hoda said he died of a heart attack on the Wednesday morning, that the funeral Mass was at Our Lady of Lebanon on Hicks Street, and said that members of the Damour fraternity came (our translation). The Brooklyn Times Union says St. Mary’s Syrian Roman Catholic Church on Hicks Street; we take these to be one church under two names.

The same fraternity may show up two years earlier. On the evening of Monday 28 April 1930, the Damouri Fraternity Society met at Columbus Hall and chose its officers, and its treasurer is printed as “Yusuf al-Khoury Awn” (Al-Hoda, 23 May 1930; our translation). It is probably Joseph, since the name matches the one in his notices and he was a Damour man, but nothing on the page says so, and the other Awns elected with him are named without any link to him. If it was him, he was trusted with the Damour men’s money at the age of sixty.

Three weeks before that meeting, on 7 April 1930, the census taker found the family at 466 78th Street. Joseph is written “Awn, Joseph,” 60 and retired, and his wife is written “Jassin,” 50. Six of their children are on the sheet: John, 26, managing a dry goods store; Theresa, 23, a clerk in the same trade; George, 21, managing a ladies’ apparel store; Mitchell, 20, a clerk in a ladies’ apparel store; Louis, 18; and Anthony, 16, still at school. All are written “Awn,” all were born in Syria, and all give 1920 as the year they came. Matilda is not in this household.

One more page may tell us about his own family. In March 1926 the Damour magazine Ash-Shams printed a notice of the death in Brazil of “the late Jibrail,” who had emigrated about 1911, and it condoled his two brothers, “Khawaja Mikhail in Damour and Khawaja Yusuf in New York” (our translation). The notice is addressed to a Mikhail al-Khoury Boutros Awn, and Joseph’s father is Peter (Butrus) on his certificate. So Joseph may have had a brother who stayed in Damour and another who went to Brazil. This is the only record that says so, and no other record names either brother. A second Ash-Shams page, of July 1934, mentions the family of a “late Jibrail al-Khoury Awn” in Brazil, but it names none of Joseph’s family.

His wife Assin Aoun was written Essine on the ship list, Aseen in the 1925 census and Affin in one 1932 notice. Her death certificate gives her father as Joseph Segan (Segen; Sajaan in Arabic) and her mother as Susanne, with a family name that appears to read Nahmir. She died on Wednesday 16 October 1935 at Bay Ridge Hospital, a widow living at 1047 75th Street, of a ruptured appendix after an emergency appendectomy. Her son John asked for the burial permit. A Mass was said for her at Our Lady of Lebanon on Friday 18 October, the day she was buried at St. John’s, and Al-Hoda called her “the late Assin Yusuf al-Khoury Awn of Damour,” which probably puts her husband’s name after hers, the usual way in these notices. Her age is 45 on the ship list, 40 in the 1925 census and 52 in the Times Union notice (the certificate’s own age is hard to read), so her birth year is unknown.

Her notice reads like a map of the neighborhood. It names her children and their families, the sons of Habib Aoun (Sajaan, Khalil, Farid, Sa’id and Shaker), a widow, Sofia Ghareeb, and the Aql family. How the Habib Aoun sons were related to Joseph or Assin we do not know. The same undertaker, Jere J. Cronin, Inc., of Atlantic Avenue, handled both Joseph’s and Assin’s funerals.

The cemetery’s own burial search says that in June 1961 St. John’s moved Assin and her son George from Section 20 to Section 3, Row C, the row where the same search places Joseph’s own burial, almost thirty years after his funeral. A volunteer’s Find a Grave listing, made in 2016, still shows Assin and George in Section 20 with three other Owens we do not know.

What we foundThe ship list of 1920 shows eight children, but the family had nine. Esther, who lived in Lebanon, is named in the Arabic notices of 1930 and 1935, and in 1961 an American paper reports her death in Beirut.

“informing him of the death in Beirut, Lebanon, of his sister, Mrs. Esther Ayoub”The Caravan, 2 February 1961

Joseph and Assin’s children were John, Michel (Mitchell), Elias (Louis), Anthony, George, Nassib, Esther, Matilda and Theresa. Esther is not on the 1920 ship list or in the 1925 census. The English death notices of 1932 and 1935 name only Matilda and Theresa as daughters, but the Arabic notices of 1930, 1932 and 1935 list Esther first among the sisters, and in 1930 as the wife of As’ad Aoun (our translation, to be confirmed by a native reader).

The Caravan of 2 February 1961 tells the rest. A cable told Mitchell Awn of the death of his sister, Mrs. Esther Ayoub, in Beirut. She was survived by four brothers (John, Mitchel, Louis and Anthony), two sisters in Brooklyn (Mrs. Matilda Gorayeb and Mrs. Theresa Koury), a son, Antoine Ayoub, with his wife and children, and two daughters in Beirut, printed as Mrs. Sira San Remi and Mrs. Mary Awn. A Mass was said for her at Our Lady of Lebanon on Sunday, 29 January 1961. The paper gives no date of death. That she is the sister the family says stayed in Lebanon is probable, and that she is the wife of As’ad Aoun is probable too, since the Caravan’s “Ayoub” fits the name Ash-Shams gives for her husband, As’ad Ayoub Aoun.

Two brothers died young. Nassib’s death certificate shows “Nassib K. Owen,” a printer, single, aged 27, who died at 37 Tompkins Place on 2 October 1924 of broncho-pneumonia, with pulmonary tuberculosis contributing; his mother is written “Aseen Owen,” and he was buried at St. John’s Cemetery on 5 October. The ship list of 1920 has him as Nessil, 22, so the certificate’s age may be a year off. Al-Hoda and Mira’at al-Gharb printed his family’s notice on 3 October: he died “the evening of Thursday, the 2nd of October,” at about half past six, with prayers on Sunday at two in the afternoon at Our Lady of Lebanon, from the house on Tompkins Place (our translation, to be confirmed by a native reader). Ash-Shams of 1 November 1924 called him “lamented for his youth,” aged 27.

George’s death came in the summer of 1930, and no certificate for it has been found, so what follows is what the papers said. In April the census taker had counted him at home, 21, managing a ladies’ apparel store. The fullest account is in Al-Hoda of 9 September 1930, which printed his portrait over a notice of the memorial Mass held about forty days after his death: a requiem at Our Lady of Lebanon on Sunday 7 September, with friends from the Damour brotherhood around the catafalque (our translation, to be confirmed by a native reader). The paper says that on the morning of Sunday 27 July he went to an outing at a lake about fifty miles from Brooklyn with a crowd of friends, after they had all attended Mass at Our Lady of Lebanon. They chose a place and sat down to eat, and then George and a companion went out in a small boat. They had gone only about fifty feet from the shore when the boat tipped. The companion, who could swim, tried twice to save him, failed, and reached the shore exhausted. Swimmers nearby shouted, the police and shore guards came, and George was found after an hour and a half, lifeless. The paper calls him a good young man of perfect character, and says the news reached his parents, brothers and sisters, who had sent him off that morning in health and joy. The lake’s name is written in letters we read as “wtsknkma,” which may be a place name; a native reader could tell us.

The family’s own notice in Mira’at al-Gharb of 30 July says he died on Sunday, 27 July, “after a sudden accident,” in a year of his age “not exceeding the twenty-fourth,” with prayers on the Wednesday at Our Lady of Lebanon and the home address 466 78th Street (our translation, to be confirmed by a native reader). St. John’s Cemetery records his interment on 30 July 1930 and his move to Section 3, Row C on 8 June 1961. Two months after the drowning Ash-Shams printed his portrait. The papers do not agree on where the lake was, on which day he left home, or on his age; those disagreements are set out below.

That weekend was a heat wave. The Brooklyn Eagle, the Brooklyn Daily Times and the Standard-Union of Monday 28 July 1930 carried it on their front pages, with lists of people who had drowned at beaches, rivers and lakes around New York, mostly on the Saturday and Sunday (one headline counted “twelve drowned”). None of the six pages we read names George, though some columns are too faint to read in our copies, a short item could be missed, and the Monday papers may have gone to press too early. They show what that weekend was like.

The family has told the story a little differently. The family says, through Jackie, that two of Anthony’s brothers died young: one drowned, and one died after surgery on his arm. The records bear out the drowning, but Nassib’s certificate names pneumonia and tuberculosis, not surgery, so the arm surgery does not yet match either brother.

What we foundNassib’s own border card shows where the oldest son was headed: Brooklyn, in care of an aunt, at what we read as 202 Baltic Street. The ship list names only an uncle back in Damour, so the card is the first sign of a relative on this side of the ocean (the aunt’s name is our reading of faint writing; the Quebec arrival sheet for the same party names a sister, Mrs. Owen, at 202 Baltic Street).

“Uncle, Michal Malleut, Damour”The S.S. Grampian passenger list, Quebec, 3 September 1920, the nearest relative named for the whole family

On 25 August 1920 the Aoun family left Southampton on the S.S. Grampian, bound for Quebec, on third-class tickets. One of the small cards from that crossing, Anthony’s identification card, is still held by the family. It carries a doctor’s vaccination stamp, the words “U.S.A. Passenger,” and a hand-written “11” after the word “line” (our reading), which would be his line on the ship’s list.

On the list, which was made out for the landing at Quebec on 3 September, the family stands together on lines 5 to 14. Joseph is written Youseuf, a farmer of 53 who could read Arabic. Assin is Essine, 45, “H’wife.” After them come Thirese (14), Georges (10), Michel (8), Elias (6) and Antoine (4), and then the older three: Nessil (22), Matilde (29) and Jean (16), whose calling is written “Carpenter.” Everyone’s last home is Damour, the destination is Brooklyn, and the nearest relative named for the whole party is an uncle, Michal Malleut, in Damour. No one in America is named. The list does not say how they were related; that the nine are Joseph and Assin’s children comes from the family and from the later records.

From Quebec they went on by train. The naturalization papers of Anthony, Matilda, Mitchell, Theresa and John give 5 September 1920 for crossing into New York State at Rouses Point, and several name the Delaware & Hudson Railway. Elias’s papers give 7 September. Nassib’s border card then fills in a little more. It was filed on the same day as the ship list. It repeats the head-tax number from line 12, 5586, and it calls his nearest relative “Michel Mallout” of Damour, probably the same uncle spelled another way. It also seems to say that his passage was paid by his parents.

Not everything adds up. Anthony is 4 on the list but 6 by his own birth date, 6 March 1914. A 1940 Brooklyn Eagle story says that John came with “his father, mother, two sisters and four brothers,” but the list shows two sisters and five brothers besides John. The count of four does not match the list, and why is not known.

Still open
The uncle: Michal Malleut is named for the whole family, and “Michel Mallout” on Nassib’s card is probably the same man. No record says who he was.
The aunt: Nassib’s card says “c/o Aunt” and a name we cannot read, at an address we read as 202 Baltic Street, Brooklyn; the Quebec arrival sheet names “Sister, Mrs. … Owen, 202, Baltic Street.” The 1920 census page for that house lists an Owen household there; that it is the same family is a lead, not proven.
The ages: six of the nine children’s ages on the ship list do not match the 1925 census. Matilda is 29 on the list, 23 in the census, and born in 1899 by her own petition, so 20 on the ship.
How many came: the Eagle’s count of four brothers against the list’s five.
The day at Rouses Point: 5 September in five of the family’s papers, 7 September in Elias’s.

On 9 November 1909, in the Supreme Court of Kings County, a merchant named Antoon Wahbeh Saydah described himself for the clerk. He was 28, white, with a dark complexion, five feet six and 170 pounds, with black hair and brown eyes, and “tattoo cross (x) on left hand.” He lived at 570 48th Street, and he signed with an x before his name. In December 1923 he described himself again for a passport clerk, and the cross was still there.

By those same sworn papers he was born in Forzul on 15 August 1881 and left Le Havre on the liner La Touraine about 17 October 1898, reaching New York on 25 October. We have not found his name on that sailing. The one line that may be him is on a La Touraine list marked as arriving on 28 November 1898: “Smyda, Antoun,” 18, single, a farmer who could read, from Beyrouth, with $20 and a “Brother, 73 W’g’ton St.” as his destination. A month apart and a spelling apart, it is not proven, and we treat it as a question for the family. If it is him, the brother is probably Khaleel, who lived at 73 Washington Street in 1900.

The 1900 census finds an Anton Saydah, born 15 August 1880, aged 19 and single, a boarder in Abdalla Kirkish’s household in Negaunee, Michigan, “selling goods from house to house.” The Kirkish wife, Sady, is probably his sister Sada. By April 1910 he was back in Brooklyn, a single man of 29 in his brother Khaleel’s household at 570 48th Street, which is what proves that the two were brothers.

His citizenship papers run from 1909 to 1914. On 8 May 1913 he petitioned as a merchant living at 5305 Sixth Avenue with his wife, Wassila. Two witnesses swore that they had known him in New York State since December 1905: Anton Simon, a commission merchant, and Willard Van Wagner, in dry goods. The petition also says “I have no children.” Their daughter Lily had been born and had died in March 1913, two months before, and we read the line as meaning living children. On 22 January 1914 he took the oath in open court, renouncing the Sultan of Turkey, and was handed certificate no. 381478, with the last digit written over. Written under it is a new address, 4721 Sixth Avenue.

One hard chapter in Antoon’s life reached the newspapers. On Tuesday 5 September 1916 a car on the way back to Brooklyn from Milford, Pennsylvania, overturned near Tuxedo Park, New York. Najib S. Maloof, of 4616 Sixth Avenue, who had been riding with friends, was thrown out and died of his injuries; the Daily Times says about an hour later in Tuxedo Hospital, and the Eagle’s 1918 report says he was “instantly killed.”

The papers do not agree on whose car it was or who was driving. The Arabic papers do not agree among themselves: Mira’at al-Gharb calls it Antoon’s car, Al-Hoda says it was Antoon’s car with Khaleel driving, and As-Sayeh calls it “the car of Khalil Saydah” (our translations, to be confirmed by a native reader), while the Eagle’s 1918 report has Antoon “at the wheel.” That the “Khalil Saydah” of one Arabic paper is our Khaleel is probable, not proven.

Maloof’s widow, Affife, asked the Kings County Surrogate’s Court on 11 September 1916 for letters of administration; the petition names three minor children and a cause of action against “Anton Saydah” for damages. On 24 June 1918 a jury before Justice Cropsey awarded her $17,500 against Antoon. The Appellate Division reversed the verdict and ordered a new trial on 14 December 1918, a request for reargument was denied on 11 January 1919, and the case appears on the court calendar for 20 January 1919. After that we have found nothing, and the Arabic papers of 1916 to 1919 do not report the trial.

Maloof’s funeral was held from his home with special services, and he is on a Find a Grave listing at Green-Wood Cemetery (a lead, not proof). A boarder called “Maloof, Nazeeb” lived with Khaleel in Manhattan in the 1900 census; he is probably the same man, but that is not proven.

Two families from two Lebanese villages came together in Brooklyn at the end of 1940.

Anthony J. Awn was born in Damour on 6 March 1914 and says he came in at Rouses Point, New York, on 5 September 1920 as “Antoine Aoun,” by train from Quebec. When he filed his declaration of intention on 4 May 1939 he was 25, single and living at 1047 Bay Ridge Parkway, and he called himself a “negligee salesman.” He described himself as dark-complexioned, with brown eyes and dark brown hair, gave his race and nationality as Syrian, and signed “Anthony Aoun.”

His draft card of 16 October 1940 names his employer as “Koury-Awn’ Inc.” at 38 East 32nd Street and gives his brother John at 1047 Bay Ridge Parkway as the person to contact. It also gives his country of citizenship as “France,” which probably reflects Lebanon’s status under the French Mandate, though no record says so. His address was changed by hand to a Marine Avenue number. We do not know who owned Koury-Awn, Inc.; it may have been a business of his brother-in-law Michael Koury, but that is only a guess. Mitchell’s card, written the same day, names a different employer, John Aoun at 82 Greenwich Street, so Anthony’s East 32nd Street employer was not John’s business at that address.

He took the oath of citizenship on 10 November 1942. His petition calls him “Anthony Aoun, also Anthony Joseph Awn,” a clerk at 123 Marine Avenue, married to Helen (born 9 January 1917 in Brooklyn), with a son, Joseph, born 14 November 1941, and asks that his name become Anthony Awn. It gives the marriage date as 29 December 1940; the wedding section has the dates that disagree. His witnesses, Louis Glayat and Jerome Kinberg, both salesmen, swore they had known him since 1 January 1933.

By 1946 a shop called Awn’s Specialty Shop, at 82 Greenwich Street near Rector Street in Lower Manhattan, was advertising for a salesgirl in lingerie and sportswear, and it kept advertising in 1949, 1965 and 1984. The 1950 census calls Anthony a “comptroller” in retail ladies’ wear, and his 1966 obituary calls him an associate in the shop. The family says the brothers began with a fabric store and that Anthony, John, Elias, Theresa and Matilda all worked in the business (the family’s account).

He was also active in the community. The Caravan, the Lebanese-American newspaper, lists a “Mitchell Awn” and an “Anthony Awn” among the officers of the St. Nicholas Young Men’s Club in 1953 and 1954, and pictures him as its president in November 1955, with a re-election notice in February 1956. His 1966 obituary calls him a past president of the St. Nicholas Young Men’s Association, which fits the 1955 and 1956 items that name him beside his photograph as president; that the 1953 and 1954 officers are our Mitchell and Anthony is probable. A “Tony Awn” won the Class B golf championship at the Syramar Golf Club in 1963, probably him. The family belonged to Our Lady of Lebanon, the Maronite church in Brooklyn Heights, where the family’s funerals were held in the 1920s and 1930s when it stood at 295 Hicks Street; Joseph’s wedding there in 1967 is on record.

  • Joseph A. Awn’s death date. The family’s memorial card says 18 January 1989; Find a Grave gives 20 January. The cemetery’s burial record and a January 1989 death notice would settle it.

  • Esther Ayoub’s family in Beirut. The 1961 Caravan notice names her son and two daughters in Beirut. It names no husband; Arabic papers of 1930 name As’ad Aoun as Esther’s husband (our translation). We do not have her birth, marriage or the date of her death in January 1961. Does anyone remember them?

The Saydah family

  • Antoon’s birth year. His own papers say 15 August 1881; the 1900 census and his ship’s age of 18 fit 1880; one 1915 age points to 1878 or 1879. A Forzul baptism record would settle it, but we have found none.

  • Antoon’s ship and arrival. He swore he arrived on La Touraine on 25 October 1898. The only matching entry, “Antoun Smyda,” 18, is on a list dated 28 November 1898 and may not be him. Several records give about 1900 or 1901 instead, though the 1900 census and his 1923 passport application say 1898. We need the 25 October sailing’s page, or a record that rules the Smyda entry in or out.

  • 73 Washington Street, 1898. The La Touraine entry says he was joining a brother at 73 Washington Street. That is probably Khaleel, but we have not shown where Khaleel lived in 1898. A grocer named John Haddad is listed at 73 Washington Street in an 1898 Brooklyn directory, which looks like a coincidence of number only.

  • Antoon’s naturalization. His petition shows a certificate number of 381478, issued 22 January 1914, with the last digit written over; a handwritten number beside his name on the 1924 Majestic list is different. The certificate itself has not been seen.

  • Wassela’s birth year and birthplace. The 1900 census says May 1886; later records fit about 1889 to 1893 (the Social Security index says 20 December 1889). Is Zahleh really her birthplace? Her 1969 death certificate may give a date; we do not have it, only a newspaper index line (The Record of Hackensack, 8 January 1969) and a sympathy notice. Her death is given as December 1968 by one index and 6 January 1969 by two others; she was buried on 9 January 1969.

  • The church. Anthony’s side was Maronite, at Our Lady of Lebanon. One Arabic paper of January 1941 says the bride was not Maronite, and the Saydahs used Presbyterian and Episcopal churches (Khaleel studied at a Presbyterian seminary, and Antoon’s funeral was at Christ Episcopal). The December 1940 registers of Our Lady of Lebanon and St. Anselm’s, and Holy Trinity Church’s 1912 register, may say more.

  • The end of Antoon’s life. His South Carolina death certificate and the Eagle name Allendale; two Arabic papers say Columbia. It is probably Allendale; a clearer copy of the certificate would settle it. The church for his funeral is Christ Episcopal in two papers and a street near “76th Street and Fourth Avenue” in the third. His burial is given as Monday 21 November 1949 by one paper and 11 February 1950 by Green-Wood (perhaps a vault). We have not asked Green-Wood for the full records for Antoon and Wassela, and we have no will or estate file.

  • The Tuxedo Park accident, 1916. The papers disagree on whose car it was, who was driving (Antoon or Khaleel) and whether the others were hurt. We do not know how the new trial ordered in December 1918 ended (the last mention is a January 1919 court calendar), and we have not found Najib Maloof’s death certificate (his age at death is 50, 51 or 52 depending on the paper), the court file, or the names of the other men in the car.

  • Antoon’s brothers. Khaleel and Michael say they arrived in 1891, before Ellis Island opened, so the Ellis Island database will not have them. No village register for Forzul is in any collection we use, and the Ottoman population registers have not been searched for the family.

  • Khaleel’s birth. Records give 19 March 1870 (his death certificate), March 1868, about 1867, about 1870, 15 March 1866 and 19 March 1867 (his passport applications). His Beirut degree is dated 1887 by one paper and 1888 by two other sources.

  • Khaleel’s arrival. He says 1891; one 1915 age points to about 1903. The certificate’s 47 years points to about 1901 for New York, but he was at 73 Washington Street in 1900. His Cook County naturalization of 2 November 1896 is known from his own statements and an index card; the petition and certificate have not been seen. We have not found a will or estate file, or Green-Wood’s full records (he appears twice, 29 November 1948 and 1 October 1949).

  • Khaleel’s wife and son. We know Katherine’s family name only as Kourani or Kourany (probably). Her age is 40 on the 1915 census but about 32 on three other records (1882 to 1883). Edward’s birth certificate (Brooklyn, about August 1906) has not been ordered.

  • Michael’s birth and arrival. His own records say 6 August 1877; his sons’ certificates imply about 1869 to 1870. He says he sailed in October 1891, but the 1925 census gives about 1889 for his years in the U.S. We cannot show when he moved from Chicago to Brooklyn: he was there by September 1918 (his draft card).

  • Michael’s naturalization. His own statements say Cook County, 18 March 1903; the 1925 census says Brooklyn, 1915. We have not seen the certificate.

  • Michael’s marriages. We do not know what became of his first wife, Marie Luckey (Grundy County, Illinois, 1901; only an index line is in hand). The Arabic press puts his marriage to Zakeya in Chicago on 5 February 1909, but we have no record of it. Her age is 28 or 32 in 1909 and 1913 (about 1881) and 34 in 1925 (about 1891); her death record (Green-Wood burial, 26 February 1966) should settle it.

  • Michael’s children. His first son’s first name is Jameel on the Cook County birth entry and the 1925 census and James on the 1916 consular certificate. Which did he use? We also lack cemetery records for his infant sons Victor (1919) and Theodore (1922; Mount Olivet), and a death record for his infant daughter Samura (1912), known from an index line.

  • Michael in 1953. His 1953 notices give 8646 Fort Hamilton Parkway, probably a later address than the 8726 of 1922 to 1925. We do not have his death certificate (1 March 1953) or Green-Wood’s full record. Who one bridesmaid at a 1951 wedding was is a question for the family.

  • Murad Saydah. We have no record of when or where Murad died, only that he was alive in January 1920 and his widow Tirkman was widowed by 1929. The 1920 census puts a Murad Saydah, 48, at a jewelry store in Oak Park, and also a Murad Sayyidah, 48, at the Chicago State Hospital four days later; whether they are one man is open. His naturalization is an index card of 19 March 1895 against “about 1897” in his son Harold’s papers.

  • Arrival years for Murad and Tirkman. Murad’s arrival is about February 1890 in one paper, 1886 as we read the 1920 census, and 1891 in his son Ferris’s 1919 registration. Tirkman’s years in the U.S. are 35 in 1925 and 37 in 1929.

  • Tirkman. Her family name is written many ways (Bouinain, Bouman, Abu Aynin and others). Her age at death is 61 or 51 (48 on the 1925 census), and the day of her funeral is Tuesday 10 September 1929 in the Arabic notice and “to-morrow” in the Brooklyn Daily Times of that day. We do not know when she returned from Syria in 1927 or 1928, or who signed Nora’s 1920 papers.

  • Ferris and Fares. Ferris’s birth day is 2 or 26 February 1896, his 1925 census age is a year off both, and his death is placed at Presbyterian Hospital, Manhattan, on the certificate and in Tenafly in an Arabic notice (the certificate is probably right). The wedding of Fares and Alice is 18 December 1926 on the certificate and 11 December in the Arabic notice. His sworn 1919 application says 2 February 1896, so that is probably right; his 1921 and 1923 passport applications may confirm it.

  • Sada and Nustas Kirkish. The 1948, 1949 and 1953 notices name them as Antoon’s sisters. Sada is probably “Sady,” wife of Abdalla Kirkish, in whose Michigan household Antoon boarded in 1900. We have not found a record naming their parents. Her husband is called Abdalo, Assad, Abdalla and Abdullah in different records (probably two men); Nustas’s is “Marun,” Michael and Mike. The 1953 notice says only “California” for both sisters. Sada’s Michigan death record, and the Kirkish naturalization and death records for Marquette County, might settle these.

  • Sada’s and Nustas’s birth years. Sada’s birth is 1874 or 1875 in the census and the California death index, but 14 May 1880 in a Social Security line that may be a different Sadie. Nustas’s is 17 June 1884 in the Social Security index and about 1885 to 1887 in other records.

  • Mary Abraham. In 1920 she is the Kirkish head’s mother-in-law, but Antoon’s mother was Noor. She may be misrecorded, a step-relation, or the Sadie in that household may be someone else. Her own age differs by five years between 1900 and 1920. A question for the family.

  • The Kirkish children. Naseem’s birth is 1899 in Michigan (a birth return of 14 October 1899; the 1900 census says December); a 1922 marriage record that says “born Syria” is probably a different Naseeb. Richard’s middle name is Elias on his birth certificate and John on his death certificate (one boy, probably). The 1920 Forsyth census page reads “Sada” for the wife of a Mikel Kirkish, but the sons are Nustas’s, so she is probably Nustas under a wrong first name. Sada’s immigration year is 1891 in 1900 and 1897 in the 1920 Munising census; a 1912 entry is probably a different Sadie.

  • Evelyn, Virginia and Eli. The 1940 census sheet we found lists only Antoon, Wassela and Helen. Virginia’s marriage to George Hakim is known from newspapers and indexes (on 6 May 1945, though one paper’s “next Sunday” would give 29 April) and Evelyn’s marriages (1953 and 1971 licenses) from indexes. None of the certificates has been ordered, and we do not know where Eli Zrake is buried or who his first wife was beyond the Arabic press of October 1945. Eli’s age at death is 38 or 39.

  • Virginia’s and Evelyn’s births. Virginia’s birth is 29 June 1921 in her index line and about December 1921 by the 1930 census. Evelyn’s 1920 index line spells the family name “Saydak”. Their birth certificates have not been ordered, and we have not shown that “Evelyn Z. Deschner” in Green-Wood’s index is Evelyn Saydah.

  • The Zrake family. Matilda Zrake’s birth is 20 January 1901, 20 November 1901 or 1900, and her first initial is N in one 1962 notice. Green-Wood lists a Matilda buried in December 1954, but several later prints treat her as living. We do not know when Najeeb Zrake died, between 1944 and October 1945, or whether he made a 1914 declaration of intention in Norwich, Connecticut.

  • George Hakim. A 1956 Caravan calls George Hakim the brother of “Mrs. Anthony Awn”; our records make him Virginia’s husband, so Helen’s brother-in-law. No record explains it. A native reader should also check the Arabic engagement, wedding and birth notices for the Hakim and Zuraiq families.

The Hadad (Hodod) and Shehadi families

  • The 1892 arrival. We have not found the arrival record for John (Hanna) Hadad, Amina and the children. The censuses give 1892 for the Hadad family; Wassela’s own entries give 1893 and 1895.

  • Hanna Hadad’s birth, death and burial. His birth year is about 1845 on three records and 1850 on the 1900 census. Syracuse papers and an Arabic notice say he died at Clifton Springs; the state death index says Willard (certificate 50442). One paper says he was buried at Morningside Cemetery and Find a Grave says Oakwood Cemetery, Syracuse, with no marker. One paper says eighteen months at Clifton Springs, another eight. His death certificate and the cemetery records would settle these.

  • The Shehadis. Were they Hanna’s brothers-in-law or cousins? Two papers say brothers-in-law and one says cousins (probably brothers-in-law). One Arabic notice names a son-in-law Antoon; another names “Amin Sayda” as the daughter’s husband. Joseph J. Shehadi’s arrival is given as 1892, 1894 or 1897, and his wife Nabeha’s as 1906 or 1908. Frederick Shehadi’s age (13 months in 1907, about five in 1909) cannot be squared yet.

  • Wassela’s brothers and other Haddad men. Salim Hadad’s age is 26 on a 1913 notice but 28 or 29 by the 1900 census; his World War I draft card has not been found. A Najib Haddad buried in Syracuse (1895 to 1955) has two different birth dates, neither matching the 1900 census (October 1896); his Oakwood burial record and Watertown death certificate may show whether he is ours. We do not know where Charles Haddad was in 1900, or what became of Beshara Shahady’s family. Who the Najeeb Hadad of 4721 Sixth Avenue (died 13 January 1914) was is also open.

  • The Hadads’ 1897 arrival sheet. It reads “98 Washington Street”; the Ellis Island index card reads “89” (faint).

  • Helen Hadad and Lily. Helen Hadad’s death date is 21 April 1908 on her certificate and 12 April in an Arabic paper. Mount Olivet’s lot records may show whether she and Lily (1913) share a lot, and who bought it.

  • People at the 1912 wedding. Who “R. G. Haddad” (the physician on Lily’s certificate) and Maheeba Fuleihan (a witness) were, and whether they were relatives, is a question for the family.

The Merhige family

  • Amin and Helen Merhige. Amin’s landing is about 1893 on his declaration, about 1901 on a 1941 index and 1900 on the 1930 census. Helen’s years in the U.S. are 1895, 1900 and 1913 in different records, though she is already in the 1905 and 1910 censuses. Georgette’s birth (10 January 1910 by her petition) clashes with her census ages and with when she says she married. We have no burial place for Amin, no death certificates for Amin, Habib or Georgette, and no burial place for the infants.

  • The Merhige connection. A 1897 sheet shows two Hadads going to a “cousin Amin” at the address where Amin Merhige’s brothers were headed. It does not settle whether this Wassilah is Wassela. A question for the family.

  • The 11th Avenue house. The family says Amin built it. Brooklyn building permits and the Kings County deed index for 8314 11th Avenue should show the builder and the year.
